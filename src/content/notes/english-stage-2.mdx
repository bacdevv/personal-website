## Articles: a, an and the

### 31. The and a, an — definite and indefinite articles

Use **a / an** when you introduce a singular countable noun for the first time. It does not identify a particular thing yet. Use **the** when you return to it or mean a particular thing.

- *She's got **a** girl and **a** boy. **The** girl's ten and **the** boy's eight.*
- *He gave me **a** book and **a** cassette. I liked **the** book but I didn't like **the** cassette very much.*

**A** usually comes before a consonant sound; **an** usually comes before a vowel sound. *A sandwich*, but *an apple*. The sound, not just the first letter, determines the choice (extra clarification).

<EnglishStage2Exercise groupId="p31" />

### 32. The articles a and the

**The** also identifies a person, animal or thing that both speakers already know. It does not have to be mentioned earlier.

- *That's **a** nice car. Did you clean **the** car?* The car means yours or the one we mean.
- *We've got **a** dog. Where's **the** dog?* The dog is already known.
- *You've got **a** big garden. The dog's in **the** garden.* The garden at your house.
- *Is there **a** station in this town? **The** station is in the town centre.*
- *This is **an** interesting book. **The** author has some very unusual ideas.*
- ***The** café next to **the** station is open all night.* The description tells us exactly which café.

**Remember:** a/an + singular countable noun; the + singular or plural noun when it is identifiable.

<EnglishStage2Exercise groupId="p32" />

### 33. The — the definite article

We use **the** for things understood as unique: **the moon, the sun, the sky, the sea, the weather, the earth, the world**, and titles or descriptions such as **the President of France** or **the capital of Great Britain**.

We normally do **not** add *a* or *the* before most place names: *France*, *London*, *Africa*. This lesson asks you to rewrite **the whole sentence**, adding *the* only where needed.

## Countable and uncountable nouns

### 34. Some, any — countable and uncountable nouns

A **countable** noun can be counted: *one biscuit*, *two biscuits*, *three biscuits*. It can be singular or plural, and singular countables can take *a/an*.

An **uncountable** noun names something treated as a mass or substance: *milk*, *bread*, *toast*. In the grammar taught here, do not say *a milk*, *two breads* or *three toasts*. Use **some milk**, **some bread**, **some toast**, or a unit expression. Uncountable nouns use singular verb agreement.

| Countable | Uncountable |
| --- | --- |
| *a biscuit* | *some milk* |
| *a sandwich* | *some bread* |
| *a glass of milk* | *some toast* |

**The** can be used before either kind. The original material refers back to Stage 1, Exercise 2, which is not included in either PDF supplied here.

### 35. Counting the uncountables

To count an uncountable substance, count a **container**, **measure** or **piece** instead.

- **Measures:** *a litre of milk*, *two kilos of potatoes*.
- **Containers:** *a bottle of water*, *three jars of jam*.
- **Pieces:** *two slices of bread*, *a piece of chocolate*.
- **Things you buy:** *a loaf of bread*, *a bar of chocolate*.

*I'd like a glass of water, please.* / *Did you buy a bag of sugar?*

### 36. A, an, some

Choose the word by the kind of noun:

- **a / an** + singular countable noun: *a sandwich*, *an egg*.
- **some** + plural countable noun: *some eggs*.
- **some** + uncountable noun: *some bread*.

Compare: *Can I have **a** sandwich?* / *I usually have **an** egg for breakfast.* / *There are **some** eggs in the fridge.* / *We've got **some** bread.*

<EnglishStage2Exercise groupId="p36" />

### 37. Some and any — positive, negative and questions

Use **some** in most positive statements and **any** in ordinary negative statements and questions with plural countable or uncountable nouns.

| Sentence | Singular countable | Plural countable | Uncountable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Positive | *an orange* | *some oranges* | *some bread* |
| Negative / question | *an orange* | *any oranges* | *any bread* |

- *I've got **an** orange / **some** oranges / **some** bread.*
- *Have you got **any** oranges?* / *I haven't got **any** bread.*
- *Have you got **an** orange?* (Singular remains *an*, not *any*.)

**Important exception:** In offers and requests, the source usually uses **some**, even in a question: *Would you like some oranges?* / *Can I have some bread?*

## Adverbs of manner

### 38. Quickly, slowly, badly — adverbs of manner

An **adjective** describes a noun. An **adverb of manner** describes how someone does something.

- *She's a slow learner* → *She learns **slowly**.*
- *She's a careful driver* → *She drives **carefully**.*
- *He's a quick worker* → *He works **quickly**.*

Most manner adverbs use **adjective + -ly**: *beautiful → beautifully*, *careful → carefully*, *quick → quickly*. For adjectives ending in consonant + **y**, change **y → i** before **-ly**: *angry → angrily*, *happy → happily*.

**Irregular or unchanged forms:** *good → well*, *hard → hard*, *fast → fast*, *late → late*, *early → early*. Do not assume that every adverb ends in *-ly*.

**Word order:** Put a manner adverb after the verb (*She drives carefully*) or after the verb and its object (*You speak Spanish well*).

## Comparative adjectives

### 39. Bigger than, more expensive than — comparatives with -er and more

Use a **comparative** to compare two people or things. The word **than** introduces the second item.

| Type | Rule | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short, usually one syllable | **-er + than** | *cheap → cheaper than*, *clean → cleaner than*, *dark → darker than* |
| Short adjective ending vowel + consonant | Double consonant + **-er** | *big → bigger than*, *hot → hotter than* |
| Two syllables ending in consonant + y | **-ier + than** | *pretty → prettier than*, *happy → happier than*, *dirty → dirtier than* |
| Many other two-syllable adjectives | **more + adjective + than** | *more careful than*, *more worried than* |
| Three or more syllables | **more + adjective + than** | *more expensive than*, *more intelligent than*, *more beautiful than*, *more interesting than* |
| Irregular | Learn the form | *good → better than*, *bad → worse than* |

Examples from the lesson: *This restaurant is **more expensive than** the one across the road.* / *Sue's **friendlier than** her sister.* / *Please be **more careful**.* / *She's five years **older than** her husband.*

**Extra comparative notes from the PDF:**

- Add **a bit**, **a lot**, or **much** before the comparative: *She's a lot happier now*; *I'm a bit older than you*; *This book is much more interesting than the other one*.
- After **than**, you may use an object pronoun: *You're taller than me*; *They arrived earlier than us*.
- You may also use a subject + auxiliary: *You're taller than I am*; *They arrived earlier than we did*.

## Common mistakes

- **a bread** → *some bread* or *a slice of bread*, depending on meaning.
- **any bread** in an ordinary positive statement → usually *some bread*.
- **a apple** → *an apple*.
- **She sings beautiful** → *She sings beautifully*.
- **He works hardly** to mean *hard work* → *He works hard*.
- **more cheaper** → *cheaper*.
- **more good** → *better*.
- **biger** → *bigger*.

## Summary and review

**Articles:** *a/an* introduces a singular countable item; *the* identifies a known or unique one. **Quantity:** count a *bottle, slice, tin, tube* or other unit of an uncountable substance. **Some / any:** normally positive / question or negative, with *some* often used for offers. **Adverbs:** *-ly* usually describes how; remember irregular forms. **Comparisons:** short *-er*, longer *more*, followed by *than*.
